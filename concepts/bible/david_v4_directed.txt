[calm, storytelling] Around 1000 BC, a prophet went to the most powerful king in Israel and told him a story about a stolen lamb.

The king was David. Months earlier, he had taken another man's wife. When she became pregnant, he had her husband sent to the front of the battle to die. [short pause] Then he married her, and life went on.

The prophet, Nathan, didn't mention any of it. [short pause] He just told a story.

[warm, gentle] A rich man with huge flocks needed to feed a guest. Instead of taking one of his own animals, he took his poor neighbor's only lamb. A lamb that ate from the poor man's plate… and slept in his arms, like a daughter.

[serious] David was furious. He said, [slowly, with restrained anger] "As the Lord liveth… the man that hath done this thing shall SURELY die." [short pause] And because the man had no pity, he would pay for that lamb four times over.

[long pause] [quietly, steady] Then Nathan said… "Thou art the man."

[long pause] [conversational, close] We still do this. We can see the stolen lamb in everyone's life but our own. A friend tells you about her situation, and in one minute you know exactly what she should do. Then the same thing happens to you, and suddenly it's complicated. [short pause] Suddenly there were reasons.

And when someone says it to our face, we defend it. "It's not like that." "You don't know what I was dealing with." A direct accusation gives us something to fight. That's why Nathan told a story first. David couldn't argue with a verdict he had just given himself.

[thoughtful] But seeing it wasn't the end. Plenty of people are shown the truth and still make excuses. David was the king. He could have argued. [short pause] Instead he said, [softly] "I have sinned against the Lord," and he didn't add a single reason after it.

[long pause] [quietly] Nathan could make him see it. [short pause] Only David could OWN it.
